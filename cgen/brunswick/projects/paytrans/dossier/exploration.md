Brunswick Pay Transparency — Exploratory Design Studio
Non-binding. Provisional. Written to be argued with.
0. What I think this project actually is
The digest reads as a communication project that grew a decision-quality objective late. I want to invert that reading.
I think this is a decision project wearing a training costume, and the conversation content is downstream of it.
The organizing sentence I'd build the whole thing around:
You cannot have a good pay conversation about a pay decision nobody wrote down.
Every hard moment in this corpus resolves to the same root. Role Play 5 fails because there's no recorded rationale to fall back on. The FAQ escalates to HR precisely at the point where the sanctioned explanations run out. EUPTD's burden-of-proof flip is the same failure with a legal clock attached. The team's own note — "what is the justification?" — is this question asked out loud.
So the conversation is not the performance. The conversation is the audit of a decision made months earlier. Training managers to talk better without changing what they record is teaching people to improvise receipts.
This reframe has a real consequence: it changes what the course teaches first, and it makes the smallest behavior in the whole project the most valuable one (see §3).
1. The fork that determines everything
Open Question 1 in the digest is not one question among sixteen. It is the fork.
Does a documented, objective set of criteria for within-grade pay positioning exist today?
Fork A — the criteria exist or will be authored before build. You can build a decision-quality course. Managers learn a framework, apply it, get feedback against it. Normal instructional design.
Fork B — they don't exist and won't in time. Then a course that teaches "apply pay principles to inform better decision making" is teaching a framework that doesn't exist, and the learner will notice inside four minutes. Fork B is not a smaller version of Fork A. It's a different intervention.
Here is the useful part: there is a design that works under both forks, and it's probably the right design under Fork A anyway. Instead of teaching the criteria, teach a method of reasoning and an honest protocol for its limits:

1. Locate the person in range.
2. Reconstruct the reason from what's actually knowable.
3. Say the part that is defensible.
4. Name the boundary of what you know.
5. Route what's past the boundary, and record what you learn.

Under Fork A, step 2 has a framework to lean on. Under Fork B, step 2 leans on evidence and step 4 does more work. The taught behavior is identical. The course doesn't have to be rebuilt when the criteria land.
I'd push hard for this, because it also removes the single most dangerous failure mode in the project (§6).
2. The contradiction managers will feel in the room
The digest names it: transparency rhetoric next to "we do not recommend proactively providing this information."
The instinct will be to smooth this. Don't. Learners resolve unacknowledged contradictions by believing the restrictive instruction and quietly discarding the aspirational one, and they discount the whole course while they're at it.
The fix is not to hide it. It's to give it a principled shape and say it in the first five minutes:
Brunswick is transparent about how pay is decided. It is controlled about individual numbers and comparisons.
That is a coherent posture. It's defensible, it maps precisely to what the FAQ actually authorizes, and it gives the manager a rule they can operate under pressure instead of a vibe they have to interpret. Process is open. Individual data is not yours to disclose.
Stated plainly, the contradiction stops being hypocrisy and becomes a boundary. Left unstated, it reads as a company that says "open conversations" and means "deflect politely."
This may be the highest-leverage single move in the project. It costs ninety seconds of runtime.
Caveat worth surfacing to the client: EUPTD partially dissolves this boundary by statute. Gender-split averages for equal-value work are, by design, comparative disclosure. The posture survives, but only if someone decides where the line moves after June 2026, and the corpus contains no such decision.
3. The one behavior I'd trade most of the course for
Write one line of reason at the moment of the decision.
Not later. Not in the conversation. At the offer, at the merit allocation, at the promotion, in the field where it lives.
"Placed at 88% — 12 years in role, sole person certified on the Navico calibration rig, top-quartile Achieve three years running."
Why this is the best available candidate for the course's core behavior:

* It's the only behavior that serves conversation quality, pay equity, EUPTD defensibility, and manager confidence with one action.
* It's small, observable, and auditable. You can measure it in Workday without inventing an instrument. Compare that to the confidence survey.
* It survives manager turnover. The current model requires a manager to recall a twenty-year wage history from memory, which is a competence ceiling almost nobody clears (digest §7.11).
* It changes decisions, not just descriptions of decisions. Having to write the reason is what exposes the absence of one. Managers who can't finish the sentence discover that in private, at the moment they can still fix it, rather than in a conversation with an employee.

If the course produced only this, it would have earned its runtime.
Dependency: somebody has to make the field exist and decide whether it's required. That's a Comp/Workday decision, not an ID decision, and it should be raised this week rather than at script review.
4. Conceptual chunks that are actually load-bearing
Not the philosophy pillars. These:
The two rulers. Grade range (min–mid–max, internal) and market range (25th–75th, external) are two different measuring sticks laid on top of each other. The role plays use the distinction constantly and the corpus never defines it for the learner. This is a genuine conceptual gap and it takes one good visual to close. Nearly every downstream confusion — "am I underpaid," "why is the midpoint good news," "when does lump sum apply" — is a two-rulers confusion.
Median is a position, not a promise. The 50th-percentile target says where the structure is calibrated. It does not say where any individual should sit. If everyone sat at median, the range would have no function. Midpoint means fully competent and experienced in this job, which is a compliment delivered in a form that sounds like a C grade. Say it that way.
Grades measure the job, not the person, and not the workload. The "does adding tasks raise my grade" question has the counterintuitive answer usually no, and it recurs because people reason from effort. A longer to-do list is not a bigger job. Scope, accountability, and complexity are what move a grade. This one deserves a worked example, not a bullet.
Burden of proof flipped. Pre-EUPTD, an employee had to demonstrate unfairness. Post-EUPTD, the employer demonstrates fairness, on a two-month clock. That is the entire reason §3 exists, and managers will not adopt the recording behavior unless they understand why it suddenly matters.
The boundary and the route. What I can explain, what I must not promise, what I hand off, and how fast. Currently encoded implicitly across a FAQ table. It should be a single explicit rule.
5. Three architectures worth arguing about
A. "Two Rulers and a Receipt" — concept-first
Structure → position → reason → record → boundary → route. Conversations appear as the assessment layer rather than as content. Strong: clean, cheap to author, fixes the actual knowledge gaps, sequences correctly (decision before conversation). Weak: opens conceptually, which is the least engaging entry point, and risks feeling like a comp lecture to a skeptical audience.
B. "The Four Moments" — scenario-first
Organized around when a manager is actually exposed: the offer, the merit cycle, the ambush ("I saw the posting"), the formal request. Concepts pulled in on demand. Strong: highest transfer, matches how managers encode, makes runtime feel shorter than it is. Weak: more expensive, harder to keep consistent, and the concepts get taught four times in fragments unless carefully controlled.
C. "Decide / Record / Explain / Escalate" — loop-first
One four-step loop taught once, rehearsed in three contexts of rising difficulty. Strong: lowest cognitive load, fits 30–40 minutes honestly, and generalizes to cases the content never anticipated — which matters enormously given how much of this corpus is unsettled. Weak: thin on compliance surface. Which is fine, because that surface belongs to HR, not managers (§7).
My lean: C as the spine, B as the practice layer, A's concepts distributed as just-in-time explainers rather than a front-loaded module. Concept-first will lose this audience in the first three minutes, and the audience is already reported as low-confidence and avoidant.
D. The honest alternative: this might not be a course
Under Fork B, the intervention that would actually change behavior is:

* a written within-grade positioning criteria framework (Comp)
* a one-page manager decision aid
* a required rationale field in the comp workflow
* a published HRBP escalation SLA
* a twelve-minute orientation explaining the above

That is a policy and tooling package with a thin training wrapper. It's less impressive and probably more effective, and someone should say so before forty minutes of e-learning gets built on top of an undefined standard.
6. Design risks I'd escalate rather than absorb
1. Fluency without substrate is worse than the status quo. A trained manager produces articulate, confident, post-hoc rationales. Under a regime where the employer bears the burden of proof, a fluent unsupported explanation is a worse position than an honest "I'll find out and come back to you." The course must not optimize for smoothness. In places it should deliberately reduce confidence — specifically, confidence in the belief "I can explain any position off the top of my head."
2. Confidence measurement will actively mislead. Confidence and competence dissociate hard here, and a course that raises confidence without raising decision quality produces exactly the failure in risk 1, then reports success. If confidence is measured at all, pair it with calibration: ask for the answer and how sure they are, and score the gap.
3. The training becomes evidence. In an EU works-council or litigation context, what the company told managers about pay is discoverable. Every approximation in the script is a future exhibit. This raises the review bar above normal e-learning and argues for legal review of the manager-facing language specifically, not just the EUPTD module.
4. Two known content defects will teach wrong answers. The lump-sum trigger (market max vs range max) and three-versus-four pillars are not editorial nits. A manager states whichever version they were taught, to an employee who may later be told the other. These block authoring.
5. Dirty data breaks the first taught behavior on first use. We're about to teach "locate the person in range." If Workday comp data cleanup is still in flight, some managers get a wrong answer the first time they try, and the course loses credibility permanently. Verify before launch, and consider a lightweight "if the data looks wrong, here's what to do" branch.
6. Works councils vanished between November and January. In several EU jurisdictions, consultation rights cover exactly this kind of change. Their disappearance from the stakeholder table reads as simplification and may be a gating dependency. This is a project risk, not a design problem, and it should go back to the client rather than be quietly designed around.
7. Training may function as a discovery mechanism. If the course genuinely gets managers to test whether their people's positions are explainable, some meaningful number will find that they aren't. There is currently no intake path for that. Design it or expect it to arrive at HRBPs unannounced, in the same month EUPTD takes effect.
7. Two scoping opinions, offered as proposals rather than questions
EUPTD placement. The corpus has circled this three ways. I'd resolve it by splitting on performance rather than audience:

* For managers everywhere, EUPTD collapses to a single behavior: recognize a formal pay-information request, do not answer it, hand it off within the stated window. That's roughly four minutes, and it belongs inside the escalation module for everyone, not appended for EMEA only. A U.S. manager can have an E.U. report. A segmented build creates a maintenance fork against a legal baseline where only 8 of 27 states had transposed as of last October.
* The substantive EUPTD content — gap thresholds, joint pay assessment, equal-value categories, response letters, the two-month clock — is an HR and legal process build. It is not manager training and should stop being scoped as though it were.

Three pillars or four. The fourth pillar isn't a peer of the other three. The first three describe how pay is set. "Build trust and support a culture of transparency" describes how pay is communicated, and it's a consequence of doing the first three well and visibly. Teaching it as a fourth pillar is a category error that will read as mushy to a skeptical audience.
Proposal: teach three pillars as the pay-setting logic, and treat transparency as the operating posture around them. This resolves the inconsistency instructionally, honors the FAQ language that's already sanctioned for manager use, and gives the client something to ratify instead of a governance question to schedule.
8. Scenarios worth building (and why the existing five aren't enough)
The 2025 role plays are the best behavioral asset in the corpus and they model a virtuoso: a manager who recalls multi-year wage histories and explains survey methodology unprompted. As a standard, that lowers confidence rather than raising it. As a reference, it's excellent. Keep them for HR. Build these for managers:

1. The one you can't answer. Employee asks why they're at 82%. The manager checks and finds no recorded reason. This is the most common real case and the corpus has no answer for it. Teaching it well is the difference between a credible course and a brochure. Target behavior: don't invent, don't promise, say what's true, commit to a date, then find out and record it.
2. The two peers. Same grade, different positions, one asks. Goes straight at the within-grade gap the team named first.
3. The formal request that doesn't look formal. An email that legally constitutes an EUPTD request but reads like small talk. A recognition task, not a conversation task — and exactly what e-learning does well.
4. The gap-report ambush. Post-2027, an employee reads a published gender pay gap figure and asks "is that us, and is that me?" Guaranteed to happen. Nothing in the corpus prepares anyone for it.
5. The manager's own objection. Practice against the manager's private view — "targeting median means we've decided to be average" — rather than an employee's. Managers do not deliver messages they don't believe, and the corpus never asks whether they believe this one. Inoculate, or they'll transmit the objection to their reports with a shrug.
6. The allocation. Four reports, real data, a fixed merit budget. Allocate, then write one line each, then see how peers allocated. This is the only exercise that touches the stated goal of better decisions rather than better descriptions of decisions.

9. If I were teaching this live
I would not open with the philosophy. I'd open with:
"Think of one person on your team. Why are they paid what they're paid? Write one sentence. Now — where is that sentence written down?"
The silence in that room is the course. Everything after it answers a question the group now has, instead of delivering a framework nobody asked for.
Second move, immediately: "Some of you are about to find out you can't answer that, and that's not your fault." Absolution first. The audience is described as low-confidence and avoidant; if the session reads as an audit, they disengage in minute two and defend for the rest of it.
Third move: give them the boundary (§2) before any content. Managers relax measurably once they know what they are not on the hook for. Almost all of the anxiety in this domain is the fear of being asked something you're not allowed to answer and not knowing it until you've already answered.
10. Things that would change my mind

* If the within-grade criteria already exist in a usable form, §1 collapses and a much more conventional course becomes correct.
* If managers can't actually see range position in Workday in every country, the "locate" step is fiction and the whole loop needs rebuilding around what they can see.
* If legal will not sanction "I don't know, I'll find out" as an approved manager response, then §1's protocol is unbuildable and the course has to fall back to the closed-answer-set model, with all the risks in §6.1. Getting that permission in writing is the highest-value thing the designer can obtain this month.

```
DESIGN INSIGHTS FOR NORMALIZATION

```

Most valuable ideas surfaced

1. Reframe: the conversation is an audit of an earlier decision. Sequence decision-and-record before conversation. ("You cannot have a good pay conversation about a pay decision nobody wrote down.")
2. The one-line rationale at the moment of decision as the course's core behavior. Serves conversation, equity, and EUPTD defensibility at once; auditable without new instrumentation.
3. Name the transparency boundary explicitly: open about process, controlled about individual numbers. Converts a felt contradiction into an operable rule. Ninety seconds of runtime, disproportionate return.
4. A reasoning protocol that survives both forks of the criteria question: locate → reason → say the defensible part → name the boundary → route and record.
5. Deliberate confidence reduction in one specific belief ("I can explain any position from memory") while raising it elsewhere. Directly counters the fluency-without-substrate risk.
6. EUPTD splits by performance, not geography: a four-minute recognize-and-route behavior for all managers; everything substantive belongs to an HR/legal process build.
7. Three pillars, with transparency as posture rather than a fourth pillar — offered as a ratifiable proposal, not an open question.
8. Absolution before assessment as the opening instructional move for a low-confidence, avoidant audience.

Candidate chunks / primitives implied

* Pay-setting chain (JD → benchmark → grade → zone → range)
* Two rulers (grade range vs market range) — currently undefined anywhere in the corpus
* Median-as-position, not promise
* Grade measures job scope, not workload or tenure
* Position-in-range reason set — extensible, explicitly not closed
* One-line rationale (record behavior)
* Say / don't-promise / hand-off boundary
* Escalation trigger + route + timing
* Formal-request recognizer (EUPTD)
* Objection inoculation (manager's own disbelief)
* Merit allocation + rationale exercise
* Published-gap-question response

Where strict schemas are likely to struggle

* "I don't know" as a correct path. Most scenario components model an honest non-answer as a wrong branch. Here it's frequently the target behavior, and it needs to score as such.
* Behaviors whose success condition is stopping. Recognize-and-route ends by not proceeding. Progress-rewarding structures fight this.
* Free-text rationale writing. No auto-scoring exists. Needs model-answer comparison or rubric-based self-assessment, which most quiz schemas won't hold.
* Moving legal baseline. Country-level EUPTD variation should be a data-driven variant slot, not hardcoded text, or the course is stale before launch.
* Calibration items (answer + confidence, scored on the gap) don't fit standard MCQ components.
* Extensible reason sets. The FAQ's four sanctioned explanations plus "otherwise call HR" will normalize naturally into a closed list. That closure is the exact error to avoid.
* U.S./international structure divergence. [S3 §11] says the comparison is inadvisable, which means one global asset needs branched content at the structure layer, not just localized wording.

Questions the designer should answer next (design-specific; assumes the digest's sixteen still stand)

1. Will legal and HR leadership sanction, in writing, "I don't know — I'll find out and come back to you by X" as an approved manager response? Highest-value permission available. Everything in §1 depends on it.
2. Can managers see their reports' range position in Workday today, in every in-scope country?
3. Does a rationale field exist in the comp planning workflow, and will Comp make it required?
4. If the training surfaces positions managers cannot justify, what is the intake path — and who staffs it?
5. Will the client accept a measurement change from confidence to calibration plus a rationale-field completion audit?
6. Is the ID authorized to propose the three-pillar resolution (§7), or must it wait on governance?
7. Are the 2025 role plays reusable as HR reference material if they are explicitly retired as the manager standard?
8. Given every milestone in the corpus has elapsed: what shipped, what slipped, and what is the live deadline?
