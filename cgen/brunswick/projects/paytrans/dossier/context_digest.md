## 1. PROJECT OVERVIEW (Reconstructed)
Brunswick is a global marine company operating in more than 25 countries across several divisions (Mercury, Navico, BBA, BBG) [S3 §16, S6 §10]. Enterprise HR's Global Compensation & Benefits function is running a multi-year Pay Transparency initiative. The artifacts in this corpus represent one slice of it: an enterprise-wide, cross-division **pay education and communication rollout** targeted at HR, then people leaders, then employees.
The initiative has two distinct regulatory triggers with very different risk profiles.
The first was U.S. state legislation. Illinois and Minnesota required pay ranges in job postings effective 1 January 2025, with Illinois extending the requirement to roles merely reporting into an Illinois manager or worksite [S4 §1]. Brunswick chose to go further than required and post ranges on **all** U.S. postings, reasoning that a single standard was simpler than tracking a patchwork and would position the company favorably as more states legislate [S4 §1]. By November 2025 the company assessed that this change had "been met with little reaction from current employees" [S6 §2].
The second trigger is materially harder. The EU Pay Transparency Directive takes effect June 2026, with gender pay gap reporting beginning 2027. It shifts the burden of proof onto the employer, obliges remediation of unexplained gender pay gaps exceeding 5%, and grants employees an enforceable right to request their own pay level *and* gender-split averages for workers doing equal work — with a written response required within two months [S6 §3, §16]. This is not a posting requirement. It is a standing obligation to justify individual pay differences on demand.
The project exists because the organization has concluded that **managers are the delivery mechanism** — "managers are the most common channel for communicating pay program information to employees" [S6 §11] — and that they are not currently equipped for it. The Jan 2026 plan proposes a required 30–40 minute e-learning for global managers and HR, an optional 60-minute instructor-led session, and an optional ~10 minute e-learning for U.S. and E.U. salaried employees, sequenced HR (April) → managers (May) → employees (June) 2026 [S1 §2].
The outcome the organization is trying to achieve is stated most plainly not in a deck but in a working-team note: **"managers that are able to make better pay decisions and have effective conversations"** [S7 §1].
That phrasing matters, because it marks a shift. The January 2025 material [S2] is defensive and communication-focused — five role plays teaching managers how to respond when an employee sees a posted range and reacts badly. The January 2026 material adds a decision-quality objective: "Apply pay principles to inform better decision making" [S1 §2], and the working team suggests this "might be top of the learning objective list" [S7 §1]. The project has quietly expanded from *teaching managers what to say* to *changing how managers decide*. Those are different problems with different failure modes.

## 3. KEY CONCEPTS & DEFINITIONS (Canonicalized)
**Concept: Pay Transparency**
- **Definition:** Clear visibility and understanding for employees of their own remuneration, and of compensation philosophy, strategy, pay structures and ranges — delivered as "a series of incremental steps that provide employees with greater visibility and clarity on their pay arrangements" [S6 §11, §12].
- **Variations:** In the 2025 manager-facing material the term is operationally collapsed into "ranges on job postings" [S2 §3, S4 §1]. In [S3 §26] it expands to a four-part structure (foundation, salary transparency, annual incentives, employee visibility). The corpus does not consistently distinguish *transparency of process* from *disclosure of numbers*.
- **Primary Sources:** S6 §11–12 (most precise), S3 §26 (most complete).
**Concept: Pay Equity**
- **Definition:** Equal pay for equal contribution regardless of gender, race or ethnicity, covering base pay *and* benefits and perquisites [S6 §12]. Operationally: "ensure that employees in similar roles are paid comparably" [S6 §11].
- **Variations:** Frequently used interchangeably with pay transparency in the philosophy decks. Only S6 separates the two cleanly.
- **Primary Sources:** S6 §11–12.
**Concept: Compensation Philosophy**
- **Definition:** Brunswick's stated basis for pay decisions.
- **Variations:** **This is a live inconsistency.** [S2 §5] and [S4 §1] both state **three** pillars: market-competitive compensation through benchmarking; balance external competitiveness with internal equity; link pay to performance. [S3 §8] states **four**, adding "Build Trust and Support Culture of Transparency to Drive Better Workforce Outcomes," and also adds "Global philosophy with a local approach" under pillar two. The four-pillar version is the later corporate deck; the three-pillar version is what is written into the approved manager FAQ language.
- **Primary Sources:** S3 §8 (most recent, most complete); S4 §1 (most operationally binding, as it is the sanctioned answer managers give).
**Concept: Grade Structure**
- **Definition:** The framework assigning jobs to pay ranges. U.S.: home-grown, 24 grades, 3 geographic zones based on cost of labor. International: WTW global grade structure, ~18 grades, zones vary by country. Hourly: locally developed, no zones [S3 §11].
- **Variations:** [S2 §6] describes the 24-grade, 3-zone U.S. structure as if it were the whole picture; [S3 §10] corrects this by separating U.S. and international. Critically, [S3 §11] states that comparisons between U.S. and international structures are **inadvisable** — a constraint with direct consequences for any single global training asset.
- **Primary Sources:** S3 §11 (table — authoritative), S3 §10.
**Concept: Grade Range vs Market Range**
- **Definition:** Grade range spans minimum to maximum. Market range is the 25th–75th percentile band within it, with market median at the center. Position within the range signals different employee circumstances: low = new hire with minimal experience, recent promotion into a much larger job, new to grade via re-evaluation, or performance below expectation; middle = fully competent performers and experienced new hires; high = long tenure in role, high-demand skills, highest sustained performers [S2 §6, S3 §10, S4 §1].
- **Variations:** [S2 §6] describes a market range "for base salary" as a distinct element alongside min/mid/max; [S3 §10] omits the market range language entirely from the narrative text while retaining it in the graphic labels. The distinction between the two range concepts is used constantly in the role plays but is never explicitly defined for the learner anywhere in the corpus.
- **Primary Sources:** S2 §6, S4 §1 (the range-position scenarios are reproduced near-verbatim in the FAQ).
**Concept: Pay for Performance / Achieve / BPP**
- **Definition:** Base salary pays for core role expectations. Annual merit increases and the Brunswick Performance Plan (annual incentive) carry the performance differentiation. BPP funding is set at the start of the year against budgeted performance approved by the Board; individual payout is modified by Achieve rating [S3 §20, §23].
- **Variations:** [S1 §5] flags this as needing only "high level reinforcement" because Achieve 2.0 training already covered it — an assumption, not a finding.
- **Primary Sources:** S3 §20, §23.
**Concept: Total Rewards / Total Rewards Statement**
- **Definition:** Base pay, variable annual comp, LTI where applicable, health and welfare benefits, and benefit incentives — viewable in Workday under Benefits and Pay [S3 §13].
- **Variations:** **Material caveat:** "For international employees, the Total Rewards Statement currently does not capture all allowances and benefits" [S3 §13]. This directly undercuts its usefulness for the E.U. population the training is being built for.
- **Primary Sources:** S3 §13.
**Concept: Merit Lump Sum**
- **Definition:** A one-time performance-based payment used in place of, or in addition to, a base increase when an employee's pay sits above market. Counts toward future bonus-eligible earnings [S5 §1].
- **Variations:** **The trigger threshold is stated two different ways.** [S5 §1] says it applies when "base pay exceeds the external market rate maximum for their role." [S2 §22] has a manager tell an employee it applies "in the event you've reached the maximum of the pay range or beyond." Market maximum and range maximum are not the same number. A manager taught one version will give a wrong answer under the other.
- **Primary Sources:** S5 §1 (the dedicated artifact, and therefore likely authoritative).
**Concept: EUPTD (EU Pay Transparency Directive)**
- **Definition:** Effective June 2026 across 27 member states. Requires pay level/range in job postings; prohibits asking candidates about pay history; grants employees the right to request individual pay level and gender-split averages for equal work or work of equal value; requires gender pay gap publication for employers with 100+ employees; mandates joint pay assessment and remediation where an unexplained gap exceeds 5%; places burden of proof on the employer; entitles workers to compensation including back pay [S6 §3]. Responses must be in writing within two months, though member states may vary this [S6 §16].
- **Variations:** As of October 2025, only 8 of 27 countries had published draft transpositions, some (Poland, Sweden) more stringent than the directive [S6 §4]. The content therefore has a known moving baseline.
- **Primary Sources:** S6 §3, §4, §16.

## 4. OPERATIONAL REALITY (What People Actually Do)
**The pay-setting chain.** A job description is written and evaluated. It is matched to a benchmark job in a purchased third-party survey (Willis Towers Watson, Mercer). The 50th-percentile market data for that benchmark, tempered by internal equity judgment, assigns a salary grade. The grade plus the work location's geographic zone produces the posted range. Individual placement within that range is then a function of skills, experience, education, technicality of role, location, and performance over time [S4 §1, S2 §21].
Internal equity is a genuine override, not a formality: market data might place Accountant II and Accountant III in the same grade, but Brunswick's internal hierarchy keeps them separate [S3 §15]. Outside the U.S., the WTW methodology governs — leveling on seven factors, banding into one of six career ladders, then function and discipline selection [S3 §17].
**The posting process is manual and rigid.** Adding ranges and benefits to postings "is a manual process by HR" [S4 §1]. The grade must be final *before* posting; if the grade changes during recruitment, a new requisition must be created and reposted; grades may not be altered to fit a candidate [S4 §1]. Grades 22+ are not posted. Commission-majority roles are not posted with ranges unless a guaranteed salary exists in a transparency state.
**The annual cycle.** As survey data publishes, Enterprise Comp runs benchmarking to evaluate range appropriateness, confirm internal equity, verify job profile accuracy, set global merit budgets, and recommend structure movement [S3 §16, §22]. Merit budgets are set per country against local economic metrics. Managers make recommendations in Workday informed by Achieve ratings and budget. Outputs are merit increases, market adjustments, lump sums, and merit/bonus letters delivered alongside annual performance reviews [S3 §26]. **Pay equity remediation is being absorbed into this same cycle** — "remediate employee pay as needed – address during the annual comp planning cycle in Feb 2026" [S6 §8].
**Roles and handoffs.**
- *Enterprise Comp & Benefits* (VP Global C&B, Director Compensation, Director International C&B, Sr. Compensation Analyst) owns structures, benchmarking, calibration, and policy [S3 §7, S6 §10].
- *Division HR* (Mercury, Navico, BBA, BBG representatives sit on the pay transparency team) performs job matching with manager input in the U.S., applies practice locally, and is the named escalation point [S3 §16, S6 §10].
- *Managers* are the front-line channel and, in the role plays, are expected to recall an employee's multi-year pay history, explain survey methodology, and describe lump-sum mechanics unaided [S2 §18, §22].
- *HR ambassadors* co-facilitate the instructor-led sessions alongside the EHRC pay transparency team [S1 §2].
- *Works Councils (EMEA)* appear as a stakeholder audience in the November 2025 draft [S6 §15] and are **absent** from the January 2026 version [S1 §4].
**Decision points.** The FAQ encodes a consistent escalation rule: a manager may explain philosophy, structure, and general range logic, but must not promise adjustments. "Can I have a pay increase?" routes to the HRBP. So does any case where an employee's range position does not fit one of the four sanctioned low-in-range explanations [S4 §1]. Role Play 5 models the escalation itself — the manager cannot resolve the frustration and convenes HR [S2 §28].
**What is implied but never stated.** Who owns intake and tracking for EUPTD information requests. How "categories of workers doing work of equal value" get defined. How a manager determines whether a given report falls under EUPTD coverage. What a manager does when the honest answer to "why am I positioned here" is that no one recorded a rationale. Who signs the two-month written response. None of this appears in the corpus.

## 5. ASSUMPTIONS (Explicit and Implicit)
**Explicit**
- *Managers are the primary channel and must therefore be equipped.* "Managers are the most common channel for communicating pay program information to employees… Managers especially need to be well equipped to handle pay transparency discussions with direct reports" [S6 §11].
- *Performance-to-pay linkage is already understood.* "High level overview as this has been covered with Achieve 2.0 training" [S1 §5].
- *The Achieve rollout model transfers.* "Similar approach to Achieve Training Rollout – Enterprise, cross-division approach" [S1 §2]; "Roll out training and communication once Achieve 2.0 and Compensation planning processes are complete – similar approach to Achieve 2.0 rollout" [S6 §8].
- *An accurate job description is the load-bearing foundation.* "An accurate job description is the foundation of determining the correct pay for a role which cascades to pay equity" [S6 §11].
- *Content must be stakeholder-specific.* "Develop training materials that are specific to each stakeholder group" [S6 §8].
**Implicit**
- *That a knowledge gap is the binding constraint.* The entire intervention is educational, yet [S7 §1] describes the problem as absent objective criteria and unused competency-based appraisal — a process and tooling gap. Training cannot supply a criteria framework that has not been written.
- *That underlying compensation data is clean enough to withstand scrutiny.* Contradicted by concurrent workstreams: "Ensure accuracy and completeness of compensation and benefit data in Workday"; "comp plan clean-up"; "Continue adding compensation plans in Workday for a true picture of total rewards in the E.U." [S6 §7, §8]. The training assumes a manager who checks range position will get a correct answer.
- *That existing pay decisions are in fact defensible.* Every role play presumes a correct, explainable answer exists. The pay equity analysis and Feb 2026 remediation [S6 §8] imply some are not.
- *That the U.S. non-reaction predicts the E.U. reaction.* [S6 §2] treats "little reaction" as reassuring. Passive visibility of a range and an enforceable right to gender-split comparative data are structurally different events.
- *That managers can access and hold the required context.* The role plays have managers reconstructing 20-year wage histories and hiring-decision rationale from memory [S2 §18, §27].
- *That employees will accept market-and-range explanations.* Four of five role plays resolve in acceptance. Practice 5 is the only one where frustration persists, and it resolves by escalation rather than by resolution [S2 §28].
- *That optional employee training will be consumed.* Only the manager/HR e-learning is marked Required; the employee module and the ILT are Optional [S1 §2] — yet employee understanding is the stated June outcome [S1 §4].
- *That U.S.-framed content transfers to EMEA.* The role plays and FAQs reference dollars, Glassdoor, U.S. states, and the U.S. 24-grade structure — while [S3 §11] states U.S./international structure comparisons are inadvisable.
- *That EUPTD content can be appended.* Three unreconciled placements: "separate from this process" [S1 §2]; "10 minutes of EUPTD content at the end of the training"; or a segmented e-learning delivered only to managers with E.U. employees [S7 §1].
- *That confidence is a proxy for competence.* All three proposed measures — embedded quiz, post-ILT confidence survey, Glint sentiment item [S1 §2] — measure knowledge recall or self-reported comfort, against a goal of better decisions.

## 6. SIGNAL VS NOISE ASSESSMENT
**High signal — should heavily influence downstream decisions**
- **[S4 §1] FAQs_Job Postings** (2,934 words, 1 large table; flagged high). The densest artifact in the corpus and the only place containing *sanctioned verbatim manager language*. It functions as the de facto content specification. Its appendix table of state-by-state regulations is the authoritative U.S. compliance reference.
- **The Stakeholders & Messaging table**, appearing near-identically in both [S1 §4] and [S6 §15]. Cross-source repetition of a table is the strongest available signal in this corpus. This is the highest-confidence statement of audience, goal, message, and timing.
- **[S2 §13–28] the five role plays** (~2,200 words; four sections flagged high). The most behaviorally specific content and the organization's own model of a good conversation. Whatever the new build looks like, this is the existing standard it will be measured against.
- **[S7 §1] Pay Transparency Team comments** (362 words, flagged high). The smallest substantive artifact and arguably the most valuable — unpolished, first-person, and the only source that names the actual problem rather than the intended solution.
- **[S1 §2, §5]** the current plan and objectives. Supersedes earlier planning content.
- **[S3 §11] grade structure comparison table** and **[S6 §3, §16]** EUPTD mechanics.
**Low signal — likely deprioritizable without material loss**
- Section dividers, blank slides, and copyright/page-number extraction artifacts. Roughly 35 sections are flagged low, and a large share contain no content at all.
- **[S3 §17, §18] WTW leveling and banding methodology.** Compensation-professional depth. Relevant to the HR track at most; near-zero manager utility.
- **[S3 §30] "Strawman for Decks."** Superseded by the January 2026 plan.
- **[S3 §4, §5] Total Rewards design framework and strategic approach.** Executive-audience strategy content.
- **[S2 §4] and [S2 §9]** are duplicates of one another.
- **[S3 §12] TDC composition by grade.** Abstract and potentially sensitive for a manager audience.
**A caution about what "low signal" means here.** The density metric measures extracted words, not importance. Several sections score low because their content is locked in graphics the extractor could not read: the Annual Compensation Calendar [S2 §8, 3 words], the Pay Transparency Timeline [S6 §14, 9 words], the Sample EUPTD Response Letter [S6 §17, 9 words], and the TDC composition chart [S3 §12]. These are **content holes, not low-value content**, and at least the sample response letter is likely to be operationally essential. They should be retrieved from the source files before design begins.

## 7. AREAS OF MISALIGNMENT, TENSION, OR RISK
**1. The compensation philosophy has two canonical versions.** Three pillars in [S2 §5] and [S4 §1]; four in [S3 §8]. The training's central organizing content is not settled. This must be resolved before anything is authored, because it determines the module structure.
**2. The merit lump-sum trigger is stated inconsistently.** Market rate maximum [S5 §1] versus range maximum [S2 §22]. A manager will state whichever version they were taught, to an employee who may later be told the other.
**3. Transparency rhetoric sits directly against a containment posture.** "Building trust through effective communication about compensation" [S2 §1] and "open, informed compensation discussions" [S2 §2] appear alongside "we do not recommend proactively providing this information" and "we do not share exact grades publicly" [S4 §1]. Managers will feel this contradiction live in the room, and learners generally resolve such contradictions by trusting the restrictive instruction and disbelieving the aspirational one.
**4. Grade confidentiality is not obviously survivable under EUPTD.** The FAQ withholds grade information to "maintain confidentiality and protect privacy" and to keep discussions focused "on performance and development, not comparisons" [S4 §1]. EUPTD grants an explicit right to gender-split average pay for workers doing work of equal value [S6 §3] — a right whose entire purpose is comparison. No source reconciles these.
**5. Training is being asked to compensate for structural gaps.** The team's own diagnosis: managers don't use competency-based appraisals; they need objective criteria; the justification for within-grade differentiation needs to be defined [S7 §1]. Meanwhile the data is being cleaned [S6 §8], pay equity analysis is still running, and posting automation does not exist [S4 §1]. Several of these are prerequisites to the training's premise, not parallel workstreams.
**6. EUPTD placement is unresolved across three sources.** Separate from the e-learning [S1 §2]; ten minutes appended to the ILT [S7 §1]; or a segmented e-learning for EU-managing leaders only [S7 §1]. Each implies a different build. This is currently the largest open scoping decision.
**7. Audience scope has drifted.** [S1 §2] scopes employee training to "Salaried Employees (U.S. & E.U.)" — excluding hourly employees, despite hourly postings carrying rates or ranges [S4 §1] and hourly grade structures existing [S3 §11]. Works Councils (EMEA) appear in the November draft [S6 §15] and vanish from the January one [S1 §4]; in several EU jurisdictions works councils have consultation rights over exactly this kind of change, which makes their removal a risk rather than a simplification.
**8. The timeline is compressed and dependency-bound.** HR April, managers May, employees June — with EUPTD effective June. Managers receive training roughly one month before the directive takes effect, and employees are educated in the same month their new rights begin. The whole sequence is gated on Achieve 2.0 and compensation planning completing first [S6 §8]. There is no visible slack.
**9. Measurement does not match the stated goal.** The goal is better decisions and effective conversations [S7 §1]. The measures are a knowledge quiz, a confidence survey, and a Glint sentiment item [S1 §2]. Confidence and competence dissociate readily in this domain — a manager can leave more confident and still differentiate pay arbitrarily. Nothing proposed measures decision quality, consistency of within-grade positioning, escalation appropriateness, or downstream ER volume.
**10. The E.U. Total Rewards Statement is incomplete.** It "currently does not capture all allowances and benefits" for international employees [S3 §13], while EUPTD pay gap analysis explicitly covers "all types of employee compensation, including variable pay, perquisites and benefits" [S6 §3]. The self-service tool the training would naturally point E.U. employees toward does not yet hold the picture they are entitled to.
**11. The role plays model a competence ceiling most managers will not reach.** Managers in [S2] recall multi-year wage histories, explain survey benchmarking methodology unprompted, and describe over-range lump-sum treatment accurately. Presented as the standard, this risks lowering confidence rather than raising it — particularly for the population Jill describes as already finding these conversations difficult [S7 §1].
**12. The corpus is stale relative to the present.** Every artifact predates 20 January 2026, and every milestone in it — HR April, managers May, employees June, EUPTD effective June, Feb 2026 remediation — has now elapsed. Whether the plan executed, slipped, or was restructured is unknown from this corpus alone, and that fact should gate the reading of Sections 8 and 9 below.

## 8. TRAINING-RELEVANT IMPLICATIONS (Diagnostic Only)
**Capability gaps**
- Managers cannot reliably locate an employee's position in range and articulate a defensible reason for it. The FAQ's escalation note — that if placement "does not fit one of the scenarios above," contact HR [S4 §1] — concedes that the sanctioned explanations do not cover all real cases.
- Managers lack a repeatable method for differentiating pay within a grade. Named as an unresolved question by the team itself [S7 §1].
- Managers appear unable to distinguish grade range from market range, or to explain geographic zone effects, despite both being load-bearing in the role plays.
- Managers cannot consistently sort answerable questions from escalation-only questions in real time.
- EMEA managers have no procedural competence for handling or routing an EUPTD information request; no such process is documented in the corpus.
**Knowledge gaps**
- The job description → benchmark → grade → range chain, which underlies nearly every answer a manager is expected to give.
- What a 50th-percentile target does and does not promise, and why it is not a per-person guarantee.
- Lump-sum mechanics, trigger conditions, and bonus-eligibility treatment.
- Total rewards composition, and the specific limitation of the international Total Rewards Statement.
- EUPTD rights, thresholds, response obligations, and country-level variation.
- The relationship between adding tasks and grade change — flagged as a recurring live question with a counterintuitive answer ("usually no") [S7 §1].
**Mindset and belief gaps**
- Managers experience these conversations as difficult and approach them with low confidence [S7 §1]; avoidance is the likely default.
- A tenure-equals-position belief is present in employees and plausibly in managers — it drives two of the five role plays [S2 §17, §26].
- A preference for relational and personal framing over objective criteria: "have to focus their talking points on people — move to criteria based… use objective criteria" [S7 §1]. This is a belief about what makes a conversation humane, and it competes directly with the defensibility requirement.
- A likely belief that transparency erodes managerial discretion, unaddressed anywhere in the corpus.
- Most consequentially: managers will not deliver messages they do not themselves believe. The corpus never asks whether managers find the philosophy credible, and the material asks them to defend outcomes they did not set using data they cannot see.
**Decision-making gaps**
- No shared standard for what justifies paying two people in the same grade differently — the single most consequential gap in the corpus, and the one the team named first.
- Identification of market adjustment candidates and forward budgeting for them [S7 §1].
- Bias recognition in pay decisions. Listed as an objective [S1 §5] with no supporting content anywhere in the corpus.
- Escalate-versus-decide judgment, currently governed by an implicit and incomplete rule.
- Sequencing decisions across the annual cycle — when a positioning problem should be raised so it can actually be funded, rather than surfaced after budgets close.

## 9. OPEN QUESTIONS FOR THE PROJECT TEAM
Ordered by how much ambiguity each resolves and how much rework each prevents.
1. **Does a documented, objective set of criteria for within-grade pay positioning exist today?** If not, is authoring it in scope, and does it precede the training? The team's own comments suggest it does not exist [S7 §1]. Everything in the "apply pay principles" objective depends on the answer.
2. **Given the current date, what actually happened to the April–June 2026 rollout?** Which waves shipped, which slipped, and what is the live deadline now? Every date in this corpus has passed.
3. **Three pillars or four?** [S4 §1] vs [S3 §8]. This determines module architecture and cannot be deferred.
4. **What is a manager supposed to say when they genuinely cannot justify an employee's range position?** The FAQ routes it to HR. Is that the final answer, and what does HR say?
5. **Where does EUPTD content live** — separate track, appended ILT segment, or segmented e-learning [S1 §2, S7 §1]? This is the largest unresolved scoping decision in the corpus.
6. **Who owns intake, tracking, and response for an EUPTD pay information request, and what is the manager's role in it — if any?** The two-month written response clock [S6 §16] implies a process no source describes.
7. **Is grade confidentiality still policy, and how does it coexist with EUPTD comparative disclosure?** [S4 §1] vs [S6 §3].
8. **What is the authoritative lump-sum trigger — market rate maximum or range maximum?** [S5 §1] vs [S2 §22].
9. **Has the pay equity analysis completed, and are there known unexplained gaps that managers will be asked to explain in conversations?** If remediation slipped past Feb 2026 [S6 §8], managers may be trained to defend positions the company is concurrently deciding are wrong.
